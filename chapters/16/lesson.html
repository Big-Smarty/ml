A tree can bend its boundary into many rectangles, but another family of classifiers asks a more geometric question: can we draw a boundary that leaves a wide empty corridor between the classes? This chapter builds that classifier from hinge loss, then replaces ordinary dot products with similarities so the boundary can curve.

Your milestone

You will train a linear support-vector machine on two-dimensional points, explain every update from its objective, and use an RBF-kernel classifier on an XOR-shaped fixture that no single line can separate.

1. From a correct boundary to a confident boundary

Chapter 15 classified tabular rows with splits and ensembles. Those models can express complicated regions, but they do not expose a simple notion of distance from a decision boundary. Return to a linear score, f(x) = wᵀx + b. The feature vector x has d numbers, the weight vector w has the same d numbers, and b is one scalar bias. We encode the two labels as −1 and +1. A nonnegative score predicts +1; a negative score predicts −1.

A point can be correctly classified yet sit dangerously close to the boundary. The signed quantity m = y f(x) is its margin. If y and the score have the same sign, m is positive. If they disagree, m is negative. A larger positive value means the score supports the correct label more strongly.

Calculate three margins

Let w = [0.5, 1], b = −0.5. For x = [2, 1], the score is 0.5×2 + 1×1 − 0.5 = 1.5. With y = +1, the margin is 1.5. For x = [1, 0], the score is 0, so its +1 margin is 0: our tie rule calls it positive, but it lies exactly on the boundary. For x = [0, 1] with y = −1, the score is 0.5 and the margin is −0.5, exposing a mistake.

The raw score depends on the scale of w. Multiplying both w and b by ten leaves every prediction unchanged but multiplies every score by ten. The perpendicular geometric distance from x to the hyperplane is |f(x)|/||w||₂, where ||w||₂ is the square root of the sum of squared weights. A support-vector machine fixes a score scale and prefers small ||w||₂, which makes the geometric corridor wide.

2. Hinge loss spends effort near the margin

The hinge loss for one point is max(0, 1 − m). A margin of at least one has zero loss. A correct point with margin 0.25 still loses 0.75 because it is inside the desired corridor. A misclassified point with margin −0.5 loses 1.5. The graph is flat after one and a straight slope before one, like a door hinge.

Add the regularization term

Suppose w = [1, −2], λ = 0.1, and three margins are 1.4, 0.6, and −0.2. Their hinge losses are 0, 0.4, and 1.2, so the average is 1.6/3 = 0.5333. The squared weight norm is 1² + (−2)² = 5. The penalty is (λ/2)||w||² = 0.05×5 = 0.25. The complete objective is about 0.7833.

J(w,b) = (λ/2)||w||² + (1/n) Σᵢ max(0, 1 − yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ+b))

Here n is the number of training points and λ controls the preference for a small weight vector. Larger λ shrinks w more strongly. Some books write an equivalent family as ||w||²/2 + CΣ hinge. The constants differ because one version averages the losses and the other sums them. Compare behavior and definitions before copying a numeric λ or C between implementations.

This objective defines a soft-margin linear support-vector machine. “Soft” means points may enter the corridor or even land on the wrong side when doing so yields a better tradeoff. Perfect separation is neither always possible nor always desirable: one mislabeled outlier should not force a boundary that harms every ordinary point.

3. Turn the objective into Rust updates

The reference uses deterministic stochastic subgradient steps. For a point with margin below one, the hinge part pushes w by yx and pushes b by y. Every step also shrinks w for regularization. With learning rate η, the update is w ← (1−ηλ)w + ηyx and b ← b + ηy. When the margin is at least one, only the shrinkage remains and b stays fixed. The bias is not regularized because moving the boundary without rotating it does not narrow the corridor.

let margin = point.y * model.score(point.x);
model.w[0] *= 1.0 - rate * lambda;
model.w[1] *= 1.0 - rate * lambda;
if margin < 1.0 {
    model.w[0] += rate * point.y * point.x[0];
    model.w[1] += rate * point.y * point.x[1];
    model.b += rate * point.y;
}

The two-feature array maps directly to wᵀx. A production solver might use a decaying rate, randomized order, or a dual optimizer. The small fixed loop is enough to reveal the arithmetic and reproduce the same result on every run. It does not promise that one fixed rate suits arbitrary scales. Features with wildly different units should be scaled using statistics fitted on training rows only.

Why are “support vectors” in the method's name? In the exact dual formulation, the learned score is a weighted sum of selected training examples. Points safely outside the corridor receive zero coefficients; points on or inside it support the final boundary. Our primal linear loop stores w directly, so it does not recover exact dual coefficients. It still optimizes the soft-margin hinge objective and demonstrates why points with margins under one drive updates.

Label encoding is part of the maths.

The margin assumes y is −1 or +1. Feeding 0 for the negative class makes y f(x) equal zero for every negative example, so its feature update also becomes zero. Validate or convert labels at the input boundary.

4. Curve the boundary with similarity

XOR has positive points in the lower-left and upper-right, with negative points in the other two corners. No line can put the diagonal corners together while excluding both others. One answer is to create nonlinear features explicitly. Another is a kernel: a function K(a,b) that behaves like a dot product in some transformed feature space.

The reference uses the radial-basis-function, or RBF, kernel:

K(a,b) = exp(−γ ||a−b||²)

The positive number γ sets how quickly similarity decays with squared distance. With γ = 0.5, points [0,0] and [1,0] have similarity exp(−0.5) ≈ 0.6065. Moving the second point to [2,0] changes squared distance from one to four, so similarity becomes exp(−2) ≈ 0.1353. Nearby training points therefore influence a query more than distant ones.

A kernel score has the form f(x)=Σᵢ αᵢyᵢK(xᵢ,x). The reference trains α with the kernel perceptron: visit every point and increment its α when its signed score is nonpositive. This is deliberately a kernel classifier rather than a full kernel SVM optimizer. It isolates the kernel idea while the linear path supplies hinge-loss SVM training. Only points with nonzero α are needed for prediction; the demo calls them support points.

self.data.iter().zip(&self.alpha)
    .map(|(p, &a)| a * p.y * rbf(p.x, x, self.gamma))
    .sum()

A valid kernel must produce a symmetric positive-semidefinite Gram matrix, the table of K(xᵢ,xⱼ) values. Arbitrary similarity rules need not satisfy that condition. The linear kernel is simply aᵀb. A polynomial kernel such as (1+aᵀb)² introduces interactions. The RBF kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature space without constructing those features explicitly.

Kernel prediction compares a new point with stored support points, so its cost grows with their count. The reference also computes similarities on demand. A full Gram matrix would use O(n²) memory. These tradeoffs make kernels excellent on some small and medium datasets but unattractive when millions of support points survive.

Debugging · The negative class never pushes

A CSV reader supplies labels 0 and 1. The training loop uses them directly in y * score and rate * y * x. Explain the failure and repair the boundary.

Answer

Every row labeled zero has margin zero and contributes a zero feature update. Convert zero to −1 before training, reject other values, and leave one mapped to +1. Merely changing the prediction threshold cannot restore missing updates.

Independent variation · Change locality

Run the XOR model with γ = 0.05, 1, and 20. Record training accuracy, support count, and scores for [0,0]. Predict which extremes will generalize poorly to points slightly away from training examples.

Worked reasoning

At very small γ, most points look similar, so opposing labels cancel and the boundary may be too smooth. At very large γ, influence collapses around individual samples, allowing a brittle, memorizing boundary. γ = 1 separates this fixture. Only a held-out set can choose among them for generalization.

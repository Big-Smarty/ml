PCA finds directions without labels, but it still describes one global cloud. Many datasets contain several populations: operating modes of a machine, customer behaviors, or measurement regimes. This chapter first makes hard groups with k-means, then replaces hard membership with a probability model that can score unusual points.

1. Alternate between centers and assignments

A cluster is a group of feature points that are similar under a chosen representation and distance. Clustering is not a hidden source of ground-truth classes. It is a modeling procedure: different scales, distances, and values of k can produce different valid partitions.

K-means represents each of k groups with a center μⱼ. For every feature point xᵢ, choose the nearest center under squared Euclidean distance. Then replace each center by the arithmetic mean of the points assigned to it. Repeat those two steps.

J = Σᵢ ||xᵢ − μzᵢ||²

Here xᵢ is one two-feature point, μⱼ is one two-coordinate center, and assignment zᵢ is an integer from 0 through k−1. J is called within-cluster sum of squares or inertia. It is a sum over all n points, not a mean. We do not take square roots: the nearest center under squared distance is also nearest under distance, and avoiding square roots is cheaper.

The reference and Rustlings exercise use the same primitive:

fn squared_distance(features: Point, other_features: Point) -> f64 {
    features
        .into_iter()
        .zip(other_features)
        .map(|(value, other_value)| (value - other_value).powi(2))
        .sum()
}

One full one-dimensional iteration

Take x = [1,2,8,9], k = 2, and initial centers 1 and 9. The first two values go to center 1; the last two go to center 9. Recompute: μ₀ = (1+2)/2 = 1.5 and μ₁ = (8+9)/2 = 8.5. The new inertia is four terms of 0.5², totaling 1. A second assignment leaves the groups unchanged.

Each assignment step minimizes J for fixed centers. Each mean update minimizes J for fixed assignments. Therefore ordinary batch k-means cannot increase inertia between exact steps, but it can stop at a local rather than global minimum. Initialization matters. The reference chooses deterministic feature points spread through the bundled ordering so tests reproduce exactly. Real work should compare multiple seeded starts.

An empty cluster has no mean. The reference retains its old center, a minimal safe policy for the tiny fixture. A stronger implementation can move that center to the point with largest current squared distance. Feature scaling matters too: a column measured in thousands dominates one measured in tenths unless that difference reflects the intended notion of closeness.

2. Replace a hard group with a probability

K-means says a point belongs to exactly one group and treats every group as a spherical region under Euclidean distance. A Gaussian mixture model says the data density is a weighted sum of Gaussian component densities:

p(x) = Σⱼ πⱼ N(x | μⱼ, Σⱼ)

πⱼ is the nonnegative mixture weight and all weights sum to one. μⱼ is a mean vector. Σⱼ is a covariance matrix. The project uses diagonal covariance, stored as one variance per feature. It can model a component with different widths along the axes while avoiding matrix inversion and determinants. It cannot model a rotated ellipse.

The probability that component j generated point i, under the current parameters, is its responsibility:

rᵢⱼ = πⱼN(xᵢ|μⱼ,Σⱼ) / Σₗ πₗN(xᵢ|μₗ,Σₗ)

Compute two responsibilities

Consider one-dimensional components with equal weights, means 0 and 2, and variance 1. At x = 0, their Gaussian densities are approximately 0.39894 and 0.05399. Multiplying both by 0.5 leaves the ratio unchanged. The first responsibility is 0.39894/(0.39894+0.05399) ≈ 0.8808; the second is 0.1192. Membership is uncertain rather than forced.

Responsibilities are normalized probabilities and sum to one separately for each point. They differ from the fitted mixture weights πⱼ, which describe the components before seeing this point, and from the unnormalized terms πⱼN(xᵢ|μⱼ,Σⱼ). Component indices themselves are exchangeable. If two correct runs swap component zero and one, the density is unchanged. Compare matched means or densities, not raw component indices.

fn responsibilities(&self, features: Point) -> Vec<f64> {
    let terms = self.log_terms(features);
    let max = terms.iter().copied().fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max);
    let relative_masses: Vec<_> = terms.iter().map(|v| (v - max).exp()).collect();
    let total: f64 = relative_masses.iter().sum();
    relative_masses.iter().map(|mass| mass / total).collect()
}

3. Fit the mixture with expectation-maximization

We do not observe which component generated each point. Expectation-maximization, or EM, alternates between estimating those hidden memberships and updating parameters as if fractional memberships were counts.

In the E-step, calculate every responsibility under the current mixture. In the M-step, define component mass Nⱼ = Σᵢrᵢⱼ. Update weight πⱼ = Nⱼ/n, mean μⱼ = Σᵢrᵢⱼxᵢ/Nⱼ, and each diagonal variance by the responsibility-weighted squared deviation from the new mean. These updates maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood for the chosen diagonal Gaussian family. GaussianMixture::fit performs both steps and stores the resulting components; responsibilities only inspects that current state for one point.

Update the means from soft counts

Continue the symmetric example with points 0 and 2. Responsibilities for component zero are 0.8808 and 0.1192, so N₀ = 1. Its new mean is 0.8808×0 + 0.1192×2 = 0.2384. Symmetry gives the second mean 1.7616. Unlike k-means, both points influence both means.

Raw Gaussian densities can underflow to zero when dimensions or distances grow. The project computes log density. For diagonal variance σ², each feature contributes −0.5[log(2π)+log σ²+(x−μ)²/σ²]. It adds log π, then normalizes with log-sum-exp: subtract the largest log term before exponentiating. This turns differences such as −1001 and −1000 into manageable values −1 and 0.

let m = values.iter().copied().fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max);
m + values.iter().map(|v| (v - m).exp()).sum::<f64>().ln()

A Gaussian likelihood can grow without bound if a component collapses onto one point with variance approaching zero. The reference imposes a variance floor of 0.001. A component with negligible responsibility mass receives weight zero, and all weights are normalized after the update. The reported mixture log-likelihood is Σᵢ log p(xᵢ), a sum over points. It is not averaged and should not be compared numerically with a mean loss or with k-means inertia. On the well-separated fixture, this sum rises from an early EM fit to the converged fit. The variance floor can complicate strict monotonicity on adversarial data, so tests allow numerical tolerance rather than promising a theorem for every floored update.

fn log_likelihood_sum(&self, points: &[Point]) -> f64 {
    points
        .iter()
        .map(|&features| self.log_density(features))
        .sum()
}

4. Turn density into an anomaly score

A feature point far from all mixture components receives low density. A convenient anomaly score is negative log density: s(x) = −log p(x). Larger is more unusual under the fitted model. The log transforms tiny densities into manageable values and preserves their order.

A probability density can exceed one: it is probability per unit volume, rather than the probability of a single point. Consequently −log p(x) can be negative near a narrow component; the reference prints such a value near a center. This is valid. Only the ordering of scores and a separately calibrated threshold determine which points are flagged.

K-means can also score a point by distance to its nearest center. That score ignores component spread: a distance of two might be ordinary for a broad cluster and startling for a narrow one. A mixture adjusts for each component's variance and combines uncertain memberships. It still describes only the Gaussian family fitted to the available features.

A score is not yet a decision threshold. Fit the model on representative normal training data, choose a threshold using separate normal validation data and, when available, labeled anomalies, then report false-positive and detection rates. Choosing the 99th percentile of training scores and calling it a guaranteed 1% false-positive rate reuses fitted data and ignores distribution drift.

“Anomaly” does not mean “bad.”

A rare point may be a valuable new operating regime, a sensor error, an attack, or a member of an underrepresented population. Route scores into investigation with domain context. Do not silently turn statistical rarity into a moral or safety judgment.

Density values also depend on units and dimension. Scaling a feature changes the numeric density through the variance and normalization constant. Preserve preprocessing with the model, and calibrate thresholds after the same transformation that inference will use.

The output is approximately [0.2689, 0.7311] and sums to one. These responsibilities are distinct from the input log terms and from the mixture's fitted component weights.

Debugging · Every anomaly score is infinite

A learner evaluates each Gaussian with exp(log_density), sums the raw densities, then takes -ln(). Moderate-dimensional points yield zero density.

Answer

The exponentials underflow before the sum. Keep component terms in log space, compute their log-sum-exp, and negate that result. Also floor variances so division by an almost-zero variance does not create singular components.

Independent variation · Unequal spreads

Add a broad cluster around [−2,−2] and a tight cluster around [2,2]. Find two points with equal nearest-center distance but different GMM anomaly scores.

Worked reasoning

A one-unit displacement is more plausible under the broad component because its variance is larger. K-means gives equal distance scores; the mixture should give the point near the tight component a higher negative log density. Keep mixture weights in mind: a very rare broad component can still assign low overall density.

Responsibilities are computed by subtracting the largest log term, exponentiating those differences, and dividing by their sum.

What can eighteen readings tell us?

A maintenance team must decide which machines to inspect tomorrow. Its sensor rule assigns a failure risk to each reading. On eighteen development readings from six machines, twelve thresholded decisions are correct: 1218=0.667. That number exactly describes these rows. It is only an estimate of performance on other machines.

Earlier chapters separated training loss from held-out evaluation. Here we begin one connected project that will last through Chapter 19. A row contains machine ID, day, temperature, vibration, load, operating regime, missing readings, a next-day failure label, a post-event repair field, and a frozen heuristic probability. The 288 rows are generated by a disclosed course-authored process. Shared wear creates correlated sensors; repeated machine offsets create dependence; noisy outcomes create genuine overlap; later days introduce drift. No model can infer the random part of every label.

For now, use machines 12–17 on days 5–7. The final later/unseen-machine partition stays out of these experiments. The probability column is a fixed sensor rule, not a trained model's test prediction. Chapter 13 will build a training pipeline and Chapter 14 will replace this rule with learned models.

A population is the collection named by the claim, such as future readings from new machines under comparable operating conditions. The eighteen observed rows are a sample. An estimator is the rule “count correct and divide by rows”; 0.667 is its estimate on this sample. Twelve readings from one machine would not give the same independent evidence as twelve machines.

Can repeated draws expose the variability?

Take four complete prediction/outcome pairs whose correctness is [1,1,1,0]. Accuracy is 34. A draw of row indices [3,0,3,2] gives correctness [0,1,0,1], so its accuracy is 12. The repeated index is deliberate: a bootstrap draws with replacement from the empirical sample. Keeping probability and outcome together matters; shuffling them independently would evaluate invented predictions.

One replicate draws the original number of units, recomputes a statistic, and stores the result. Repeat B times, sort the estimates, and use empirical percentiles. This lab uses zero-based indices ⌊0.025⁢B⌋ and ⌊0.975⁢B⌋, so B=2000 uses indices 50 and 1950. More replicates reduce simulation noise in these endpoints; they do not add observed machines.

A nominal 95% confidence-interval procedure aims to cover the fixed population quantity in about 95% of repeated samples under suitable assumptions. It does not assign a 95% probability to a fixed parameter after this particular interval has been computed. The tiny percentile-bootstrap example provides no coverage guarantee, especially near accuracy zero or one.

Which observations must move together?

Retrieve the distinction between a row and an independent unit. Suppose machine A has two correct readings and machine B has two wrong readings. Overall accuracy is 12. Resampling two machines gives AA with accuracy 1, AB or BA with accuracy 12, and BB with accuracy 0. Resampling four individual readings pretends there are four independent machines and changes that sampling story.

This estimator weights readings rather than giving each machine equal influence; a machine-weighted mean is a different target and must be declared.

Does “0.8” behave like eight in ten?

Recall that a threshold converts a probability into a decision. Probabilities 0.51 and 0.99 both predict failure at threshold 0.5, but they make very different frequency claims. Calibration asks whether observations assigned a probability near 0.8 fail about 80% of the time. One individual failure cannot confirm that claim.

For probabilities [0.55,0.70,0.85,0.95] and outcomes [1,0,1,1], the mean probability is 3.054=0.7625 and the observed frequency is 34=0.75. A reliability bin plots (0.7625,0.75), with count four. Binning more finely may reveal variation but also leaves fewer observations per point. Report counts; never draw an empty bin as if it were observed zero failure.

The maintenance solution's four bins have counts [5,6,5,2], mean probabilities approximately [0.1431,0.4044,0.6508,0.8255], and frequencies [0.2,0.3333,0.4,1]. These are noisy descriptions, not evidence that the highest bin is perfectly reliable.

A model can rank failures usefully while overstating its confidence. A constant base-rate predictor can be calibrated while distinguishing no machines at all. If you later fit a calibrator, use predictions independent of the base model's fitting, for example out-of-fold development predictions. Keep the final test independent of both fitting stages.

If the positive case receives 0.2 instead, its squared error becomes 0.64 and the mean becomes 0.34. This is the Brier score: sum (p−y)2 divided by the number of paired cases.

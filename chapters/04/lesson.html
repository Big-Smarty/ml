What is the difference between a score, a probability and a decision?

The weighted sum still supplies the first calculation. With features[2,−1], weights[1.2,0.4] and bias−0.5, the score is1.2×2+0.4×(−1)−0.5=1.5. This score can be any real number, so it is not a probability. We call it the logit z.

The sigmoid converts z to a probability p:

p=σ(z)=1/(1+exp(−z))

exp means the exponential function. At z=1.5, exp(−1.5)≈0.2231, so p≈0.8176. At z=0, p=0.5; at z=−1.5, p≈0.1824. Larger logits always produce larger probabilities, so the transformation preserves ordering.

A probability expresses modeled uncertainty. It is not a promise that a particular machine must fail, and a fitted value 0.8 does not establish that 80% of similarly scored real machines fail. That latter claim needs separate calibration evidence. A decision threshold is a policy: predict fault when p≥threshold. At0.5 the example becomes class 1; at0.9 it becomes class 0. The score and probability did not change.

Odds compare the two modeled outcomes: p/(1−p). At p=0.8, odds are 4-to-1. The natural logarithm of those odds is the logit, ln4≈1.386. This gives an interpretation to the otherwise unbounded score: adding a fixed amount to the logit multiplies odds by a fixed factor. It does not add a fixed amount to probability.

For numerical safety the supplied sigmoid uses exp(z)/(1+exp(z)) when z<0. Its exponential argument is then nonpositive. At large negative z, directly computing exp(−z) would overflow even though the final probability should be close to zero.

Which probability should the observed answer receive?

When the target is1, the probability assigned to the observed answer is p. When the target is0, it is1−p. A model assigning 0.8 to a positive and 0.3 to a negative assigns their joint observations probability 0.8×0.7=0.56, assuming labels are conditionally independent given their features and the model. Viewed as a function of parameters after outcomes are observed, that quantity is the likelihood.

Multiplying many small probabilities eventually underflows. Natural logs turn products into sums. Negating gives a loss to minimize; averaging keeps its scale independent of duplicated data. For those two examples, losses are−ln0.8≈0.2231 and−ln0.7≈0.3567; their mean is about 0.2899.

ℓ(p,y)=−[y ln p+(1−y)ln(1−p)]; L=(1/N)Σᵢℓ(pᵢ,yᵢ)

Here y is0 or1, ℓ is one example's loss, L is the dataset mean, and N counts rows. This is binary cross-entropy (BCE). A positive target with p=0.1 costs−ln0.1≈2.3026; p=0.8 costs only 0.2231. Confident wrong probabilities are expensive. MSE could also be used as a classification scoring rule, but BCE directly matches the Bernoulli likelihood model and gives the gradient we use next.

Why keep the logit even after computing probability?

Recall that sigmoid turns a score into a bounded value. At a large positive score, the probability rounds to exactly 1 in floating point. For a negative target, computing−ln(1−p) then attempts ln0. Even for a positive target, the combined expression can contain 0×ln0, producing NaN. The correct mathematical limiting loss and the computed intermediate operations disagree.

Combine sigmoid and BCE before evaluating them:

ℓ(z,y)=max(z,0)−zy+ln(1+exp(−|z|))

|z| is absolute value. The exponential's argument is never positive, so its result cannot overflow. Rust's ln_1p computes ln(1+x) accurately when x is tiny. At z=1000,y=0 the loss is approximately 1000; at z=1000,y=1 it is near 0. At z=−1000,y=1 it is approximately 1000 again. The calculation preserves the confident-error penalty.

Why clipping is a different operation

Clipping p to[0.000001,0.999999] prevents log 0, but caps the penalty for extremely confident errors and changes derivatives at the clipping boundaries. Stable algebra computes the original logit objective instead.

These extreme tests establish finite, sensible loss behavior. They are poor finite-difference gradient locations: probabilities saturate and tiny changes can disappear in rounding. Gradient checks use moderate logits next.

Corresponding Rust excerpt
pub fn bce(z: f64, y: bool) -> Result<f64, String> {
    if !z.is_finite() {
        return Err("logit must be finite".into());
    }
    Ok(z.max(0.) - z * f64::from(y) + (-z.abs()).exp().ln_1p())
}

Why p−y? Differentiating BCE with respect to p gives(p−y)/(p(1−p)). Sigmoid's derivative is p(1−p). The chain rule multiplies them and cancels those factors, leaving dℓ/dz=p−y. Use the simplified result directly instead of constructing and canceling potentially unstable floating-point fractions.

Transfer answer

Reversing every label reverses the preferred logit direction; the sigmoid probabilities approximately complement each other for this symmetric training setup. Contradictory labels at an identical input require the same p for incompatible outcomes. BCE must compromise; there is no deterministic feature rule that separates them.

On separable data, unregularized BCE can keep shrinking as parameter magnitudes grow even after every thresholded label is right. Low BCE is not proof of calibrated risk, and the fixed training budget is not a convergence theorem.

A classifier maps an input to a decision. A generative model attempts a different task: learn enough about a data distribution to produce new samples. There is no single recipe. This chapter trains three small models so you can compare what each objective asks the parameters to do.

Three experiments, one honest scale

The Rust project trains a scalar beta-VAE, a GAN with an affine generator and quadratic discriminator, and a timestep-conditioned diffusion noise predictor. Every model updates real parameters and samples afterward. Six course-authored values make the calculations visible; they do not make these programs image generators.

1. A distribution is more than one prediction

Our data are −1.4, −1.0, −0.7, 0.7, 1.0, and 1.4. They form two small groups around −1 and +1. A useful generator should produce varied finite values and cover both signs. Returning the mean, zero, has small squared error against some rows but destroys the two modes. This shows why a generative objective and evaluation must address a distribution rather than a single target.

The three methods expose different evidence. A variational autoencoder defines a latent-variable likelihood and optimizes a tractable bound. A generative adversarial network trains a discriminator rather than calculating an explicit likelihood. A diffusion model learns to undo known corruption steps. Their printed losses have different units and scales. Comparing the beta-VAE value 0.16 with a GAN discriminator value 1.38 would be meaningless; each number answers a different question.

A latent variable z is an unobserved coordinate used to generate x. Sampling is the path from a simple noise source through learned parameters to an observation. The project supplies deterministic noise points for training so tests repeat, and uses a seeded normal generator for displayed samples. The six equally weighted training noise values are −1.2, −0.7, −0.2, 0.2, 0.7, and 1.2. Their mean is zero, but their variance is (1.44+0.49+0.04)/3≈0.6567 rather than one. They form a deliberately coarse approximation to a normal expectation. Training therefore also differs from sampling through this noise-distribution mismatch; increasing the grid size or using repeated seeded normal draws is a useful next experiment. Determinism is an experimental control, not a requirement of the underlying model.

2. VAE: reconstruct while shaping a latent distribution

A deterministic autoencoder maps x to one code and maps the code back to x. A variational autoencoder instead makes the encoder describe a distribution qφ(z|x). For this scalar project, the encoder mean is μ = a x + c and its log variance is ℓ. The standard deviation is σ = exp(ℓ/2). We draw ε from a standard normal and write

z = μ + exp(ℓ/2) ε.

This is reparameterization. The randomness lives in ε, while μ and ℓ remain inside an ordinary differentiable calculation. The decoder mean is x̂ = d z + e. Five values—encoder weight and bias, log variance, decoder weight and bias—are trainable.

One reparameterized sample

Let μ = 0.8, ℓ = ln(0.25) ≈ −1.386, and ε = −0.5. Then σ = exp(−0.693) = 0.5 and z = 0.8 + 0.5(−0.5) = 0.55. If the decoder is x̂ = 1.5z, it returns 0.825. For target x = 1, the half-squared reconstruction term is 0.5(0.825−1)² ≈ 0.0153.

With a standard-normal prior, the scalar KL term is 0.5(μ² + exp(ℓ) − 1 − ℓ). Substitution gives 0.5(0.64 + 0.25 − 1 + 1.386) ≈ 0.638. It discourages encoding every input as a far-away, extremely narrow posterior.

The evidence lower bound contains expected decoder log likelihood minus KL. Our fixed-variance Gaussian decoder turns the negative log-likelihood term into half squared error plus a constant. The project minimizes reconstruction + 0.1×KL across the Cartesian product of six x values and six independent fixed ε values. Because the KL coefficient is 0.1 rather than one, the executable correctly labels this a beta-VAE objective, not the ordinary negative ELBO.

Finite differences train the five parameters. This is not an ML framework or hidden automatic differentiation: every parameter is perturbed by 10⁻⁵, the complete objective is reevaluated on both sides, and the central slope is used. That choice keeps the late-course miniature compact and inspectable, but it scales as two forward evaluations per parameter. An independent exercise is to derive and replace these slopes with backpropagation from Chapters 7–9.

3. GAN: train an opponent and a generator

A GAN contains two models with opposing goals. The generator maps noise z to G(z) = az + b. The discriminator produces a logit from x, x², and a bias, then a sigmoid probability that x came from data. The quadratic feature matters here: both initial real and fake distributions can have mean near zero, so a purely linear discriminator cannot detect that their spreads differ.

The discriminator minimizes the sum of the real-example mean binary cross-entropy and the fake-example mean binary cross-entropy

LD = −Ex[log D(x)] − Ez[log(1−D(G(z)))].

The original minimax generator could minimize E[log(1−D(G(z)))]. When the discriminator confidently rejects early samples, that form can provide a weak gradient. The original GAN paper proposes the non-saturating alternative used here: LG = −E[log D(G(z))]. One update changes discriminator parameters with the generator frozen; the next changes generator parameters with the new discriminator frozen.

Read an adversarial update

Suppose D assigns a fake sample probability 0.2. The generator's non-saturating loss for that sample is −ln(0.2) ≈ 1.609. If an update raises the probability to 0.4, the loss becomes −ln(0.4) ≈ 0.916. This says the current discriminator is easier to fool. It does not prove the sample matches the data distribution, because the discriminator is limited and changes on its next step.

In the measured run, the generator scale moves from 0.25 to about 1.324 and its offset approaches zero. Both networks change, and generated values remain finite. The discriminator loss ends near ln 4 ≈ 1.386, consistent with confusion in this tiny game, but the ideal equilibrium theorem assumes far more capacity and optimization than this program has. Inspect sample spread and signs as well as loss. A generator can exhibit mode collapse, sending many noise values to one region while still exploiting its current opponent.

4. Diffusion: learn the noise, then reverse it

A diffusion model begins with a fixed forward corruption process. Let x₀ be clean data and ᾱₜ a cumulative signal-retention value at timestep t. We can construct any noisy state directly:

xₜ = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ) ε,   ε ∼ Normal(0,1).

If ᾱₜ = 0.6, x₀ = 1, and ε = −0.5, then xₜ = √0.6 + √0.4(−0.5) ≈ 0.775 − 0.316 = 0.459. Training knows the injected ε. The project fits ε̂ = u xₜ + v(t/(T−1)) + c by mean squared error across all data-noise combinations and four ᾱ values: 0.95, 0.6, 0.15, and 0.02. The time feature tells one small predictor which noise level produced its input.

Sampling starts at approximately normal noise because ᾱ at the last step is close to zero. At each timestep, estimate clean data as x̂₀ = [xₜ − √(1−ᾱₜ)ε̂]/√ᾱₜ. The deterministic update then constructs the preceding state from x̂₀ and the same ε̂ using ᾱₜ₋₁. At the final transition ᾱ becomes one, so the returned value is x̂₀. This is a short DDIM-style deterministic path, following the deterministic member of the sampler family introduced by Song, Meng, and Ermon, built from a diffusion noise predictor. The stochastic DDPM sampler instead uses the reverse Gaussian transition and fresh noise before the final step.

An exact unit check supplies the true ε. It constructs xₜ from known x₀ and verifies that one reverse transition equals √ᾱₜ₋₁x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ₋₁)ε, including recovery of x₀ on the last transition. That check catches a subtle wrong sampler that repeatedly treats each x̂₀ estimate as though it were the next xₜ. Falling noise MSE alone would not catch that error.

Read the complete reference implementation alongside each objective. The measured reference run lowers the beta-VAE objective from about 0.4521 to 0.1666 and diffusion noise MSE from about 0.6567 to 0.4190. The GAN generator learns a scale near 1.324 on the deterministic fixture. Its affine mapping of Gaussian noise remains a single Gaussian family and therefore cannot represent the target's two separated modes; the run reveals training and a capacity failure together. The fixed six-point noise grid approximates an expectation rather than drawing fresh random batches. Exact displayed samples come from seed 51. Treat these as regression evidence for this program, not comparable quality scores.

The reference also generates 512 values per model using a common set of latent normal draws. It reports mean, population variance, negative count, central count |x|<0.5, and nearest-rank quartiles. For N sorted values the p quartile uses index ceil(pN)−1, with p=0.25,0.5,0.75. The data fixture has mean zero, variance (1.96+1+0.49)/3=1.15, three negative values, and no values in the central interval. Counts must be converted to proportions when comparing six observations with 512 samples. These are descriptive diagnostics against the training fixture, not held-out generalization estimates.

The central interval is particularly revealing. A centered Gaussian can match both sign coverage and variance while placing its greatest density near zero, exactly where our fixture has a gap. Affine Gaussian generators cannot repair this by longer training. The same restriction applies to the linear VAE decoder and this diffusion predictor: at each fixed timestep the denoising update is affine in xₜ, so composing the updates maps the initial Gaussian into another Gaussian. The time bias changes offsets, but it cannot create two modes. A nonlinear decoder or noise predictor is needed before fitting the separated shape is possible.

VAE sampling also requires a convention that reconstruction plots often hide. A Gaussian decoder with unit observation variance defines x=d z+e+ξ, where ξ is another independent standard normal. Returning d z+e alone gives a sample of decoder means rather than a full observation from that likelihood. The project reports both, with labels. The full observations are necessarily noisier; their theoretical variance is d²+1 under a unit-normal latent. Neither line can be ranked against GAN or diffusion by its training loss.

Practice sequence

  1. Implement reparameterization and verify that log variance zero gives unit standard deviation.
  2. Debug a VAE whose KL becomes negative. Check whether you used variance exp(log_variance) in μ² + variance − 1 − log variance.
  3. Debug a diffusion model with falling training MSE but exploding samples. Use the perfect-noise transition test before tuning learning rates.
  4. Independent variation: replace the six training values with an asymmetric two-mode set. Report generated sign coverage and sorted quartiles for every model; do not compare their raw objectives.
Hint 1

Log variance is not log standard deviation. Halve it before exponentiating to obtain σ.

Hint 2: sampler invariant

With a perfect ε prediction, derive x̂₀ algebraically. The next state must use the preceding ᾱ, not reuse x̂₀ as a noisy state.

Worked answers

mean + (0.5 * log_variance).exp() * epsilon completes the starter. A negative Gaussian KL signals an algebra or convention bug because the closed form is nonnegative. For the independent variation, keep the seed, training steps, and noise grid fixed; report mode counts and quantiles separately for each sampler.

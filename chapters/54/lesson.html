What does removing one feature explain?

A classifier has logit 0.8x₀+0.4x₁−0.2. At x=[1,1] it produces logit 1 and probability about 0.7311. Replacing feature zero with baseline zero changes the logit to 0.2, a contribution of 0.8. Replacing feature one changes it to 0.6, a contribution of 0.4.

These are local feature-ablation contributions on the logit scale. They answer how this particular model’s score changes under the specified replacement. They are not changes in outcome probability, effects of a real-world intervention, or proof that the feature caused the label. The baseline is part of the question.

With baseline feature zero equal to 0.5 instead, its contribution becomes 0.8(1−0.5)=0.4. A reader who sees only a colored importance bar cannot recover that choice. Record the input, reference value, model version, output scale, and calculation beside an explanation.

How large a nearby change crosses the decision threshold?

A probability threshold of 0.5 corresponds to logit zero. At x=[0.2,0], logit is −0.04 and the prediction is negative. Increasing feature zero by 0.25 raises the logit by 0.2 to 0.16, flipping the prediction. A point near the boundary can change class under a small perturbation even when overall accuracy appears good.

The supplied robustness function evaluates the two endpoints x₀−r and x₀+r for each observation, keeping feature one fixed. It counts examples for which either endpoint changes the original predicted class and reports the maximum probability change. At r=0.25, the twelve-row fixture has four boundary flips.

This is one named coordinate perturbation test. It does not search all directions, all feature combinations, or a perceptual threat model. For this affine logit and one-coordinate interval the extrema occur at endpoints; that argument would not establish a general nonlinear adversarial guarantee.

Input validation restricts finite features to [−5,5]. A perturbation crossing that domain must be handled explicitly rather than silently clipped or ignored. Admissible perturbations need a meaning in the application: changing a pixel, a measurement, or a demographic field are different assumptions.

Which denominator belongs to each error rate?

The two groups each contain six observations. In group A there are three positive labels, of which two are predicted positive, and three negative labels, of which one is predicted positive. Thus TPR=2/3 and FPR=1/3. In group B there are two positives with one detected, and four negatives with one false alarm: TPR=1/2 and FPR=1/4.

Positive prediction rate instead divides all predicted positives by group count. Group A has three predicted positives out of six, giving 1/2; group B has two out of six, giving 1/3. Both nevertheless have four correct predictions out of six and equal accuracy 2/3. Equal accuracy therefore coexists with unequal selection and conditional error rates. A pooled score would conceal those distinctions.

For each requested group, collect TP, FP, TN, FN. Accuracy is (TP+TN)/n, positive rate is (TP+FP)/n, TPR is TP/(TP+FN), and FPR is FP/(FP+TN). A group with no positive labels has undefined TPR, represented by None, rather than zero. Zero would falsely suggest measured complete failure to detect positives.

Six observations give weak statistical precision. One changed outcome can move a three-example denominator’s rate by one third. Report counts beside rates and quantify uncertainty before extrapolating to a population. For example a Wilson interval for 2 successes of 3 trials at nominal 95 percent is approximately [0.21,0.94]; overlapping intervals are not a proof of equal performance.

No choice of one fairness metric resolves whose errors matter, whether labels are valid, or whether groups are represented adequately. The executable checks arithmetic and makes tradeoffs visible. It does not certify fairness.

Can an attack distinguish members from easy examples?

A membership attack guesses whether an example appeared in training. This fixture trains a separate classifier on rows marked member, then predicts membership when per-example loss is below 0.45. Attack TPR divides guessed members by actual members; attack FPR divides guessed members by actual nonmembers. Advantage is TPR−FPR.

A rule that guesses “member” for everybody has TPR=1 and FPR=1, hence advantage zero. Reporting only its success on training examples would make that useless rule appear perfect. Reversing labels or changing the threshold can alter the score, so retain the two populations and decision rule explicitly.

The twelve rows are not a matched privacy benchmark. Members and nonmembers may differ in class, group, or difficulty. A lower member loss can reflect those differences rather than memorization. A stronger audit would compare matched populations, tune the attack on separate data, and report uncertainty on held-out attack examples.

Use a permutation control: keep model losses fixed but shuffle membership labels, repeating the attack calculation. If the original advantage is ordinary under those assignments, the small fixture gives weak evidence for a membership signal. The existing executable does not automatically run that statistical test, and its result is not a differential-privacy guarantee.

Which inputs should never reach the numerical model?

The request grammar is schema=1,f0=<number>,f1=<number>. Parsing requires exactly these three fields, the supported version, finite values, and coordinates within [−5,5]. NaN is especially instructive: comparisons with NaN are false, so a naive range check written only as x<−5 or x>5 can accidentally accept it.

Test malformed numbers, wrong schema, missing or extra fields, infinities, and out-of-domain finite values. Reject them before inference. This is a security boundary for one parser, not proof of an authenticated, private, or resilient service. Chapter 53 adds bounded HTTP reads; neither toy service represents a complete deployment threat model.

Privacy and security overlap but ask different questions. Access controls can prevent unauthorized requests while an authorized prediction interface still leaks a membership signal. Conversely, a model with limited memorization can still be exposed by an unsafe parser or unrestricted resource usage.

Interpretability also does not replace security validation: being able to explain a valid prediction says nothing about how malformed requests are handled. Keep evidence tied to the exact boundary that was tested.

How can every stratum disagree with the pooled comparison?

In stratum one, treated outcomes are 1/10=0.1 and control outcomes 18/90=0.2: difference −0.1. In stratum two, treated outcomes are 54/90=0.6 and control outcomes 7/10=0.7: again −0.1. Yet pooled treated outcomes are 55/100=0.55 and pooled controls 25/100=0.25, giving +0.30.

The populations have different stratum mixtures. Treated observations mostly occupy the higher-outcome stratum, while controls mostly occupy the lower-outcome stratum. Giving each stratum equal weight produces average conditional difference −0.1. This is a concrete association reversal, often called Simpson’s paradox.

Standardization changes the comparison population. Equal-stratum weights are a declared target, not automatically the right population weights. Another target population would require another weighted average. The lab validates that every stratum has treated and control observations and outcome counts do not exceed totals.

Neither the crude nor standardized contrast automatically identifies a causal effect. Interpreting adjustment causally requires assumptions such as no unmeasured confounding after conditioning, positivity for the relevant treatment choices, and a consistent intervention definition. Conditioning on the wrong variable can itself introduce bias.

A feature ablation in the classifier changes the model’s input while holding its other inputs fixed. That computational intervention does not establish what would happen to real outcomes if a person or system were changed. The distinction connects this session back to the first explanation example.
